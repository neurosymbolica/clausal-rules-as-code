four_year_term_measurement_basis EXPANSION

NOT YET REVIEWED — no qualified person has signed off this reading.

question
Art 33(1) third subparagraph reads "The term of a framework agreement shall not exceed four years". Is the four years measured on the FRAMEWORK's own term -- the period during which contracts may be awarded under it -- or on the whole period over which the framework generates obligations, so that a call-off whose PERFORMANCE runs past the fourth anniversary breaches it too?
chosen
framework_term_only
rationale
Chosen on the text: Art 33(1) limits "the term of a framework agreement", and the definition two sentences earlier tells us what a term is for -- establishing the terms governing "contracts to be awarded during a given period". A period during which contracts are AWARDED is an award window. NOTE THE DEPARTURE: the conservative reading is `framework_term_plus_call_off_performance` and this domain departs from it. The departure is the reviewable decision. NOTE ALSO HOW THE RIVAL IS REFUTED, because it is not by the battery: the rival is closed off at the INTERFACE -- no call-off performance date is a profile key, so no case can distinguish the two readings and the oracle cannot kill the rival. If a reviewer overturns this entry the fix is a new profile key and a new limb, not a clause edit.
authority
none

Readings

  • framework_term_only — TAKEN

    The limit binds FRAMEWORK_START_DATE to FRAMEWORK_END_DATE and nothing else. A call-off awarded inside the term may be performed after the term expires, and this domain carries NO call-off performance dates at all -- there is no key for one, so a producer cannot invent a limb that reads it. Matches the drafting: the provision speaks of the term of the AGREEMENT, and Art 33(1)'s definition sentence speaks of "contracts to be awarded during a given period", which is an award window, not a performance window. Risk: a four-year framework whose final call-off is a ten-year concession-like contract passes here.

  • framework_term_plus_call_off_performance — not taken more conservative

    The limit binds the whole period over which the framework produces contracts AND those contracts run, so a CALL_OFF_PERFORMANCE_END_DATE key would exist and a call-off performed beyond the fourth anniversary would breach Art 33(1). Safer against the abuse the four-year rule exists to prevent -- a short framework generating long contracts -- and much more restrictive than settled practice. Costly: it condemns the ordinary case of a maintenance contract called off in the framework's final month.

call_off_award_window EXPANSION

NOT YET REVIEWED — no qualified person has signed off this reading.

question
Art 33(1)'s definition sentence speaks of terms "governing contracts to be awarded during a given period" and Art 33(2) of contracts "based on a framework agreement". Neither says in terms that a call-off must be awarded inside the framework's term. Is there an award-window rule at all; if so, is the window CLOSED at both endpoints; and is it the term the framework DECLARES, or that term capped at the four-year limit?
chosen
closed_interval_of_the_declared_term
rationale
There IS a window: "contracts to be awarded during a given period" is what a framework agreement is for, and a rule limiting the period would be pointless if contracts could be awarded outside it, so `no_award_window_rule` is rejected. The window is CLOSED because a term expressed as two dates ordinarily includes both, and nothing in Art 33 excludes the last day; the conservative `half_open_interval` is departed from for that reason, and because it produces the absurd result that a framework whose start and end dates coincide can never be used. It is the DECLARED term rather than the capped one because the two determinations are deliberately independent: collapsing them means an over-long framework reports two defects for one wrong, and it makes the call-off verdict move when the term verdict moves, which is exactly the coupling the slice split exists to prevent. All three rejected readings are refuted by the oracle's swept case space, not merely argued against, and `sanity_check()` names the first cell each dies on.
authority
none

Readings

  • closed_interval_of_the_declared_term — TAKEN

    The window is [FRAMEWORK_START_DATE, FRAMEWORK_END_DATE] with both endpoints INSIDE, taken from the term the framework as concluded declares. A call-off awarded on the framework's first or last day is in window; one awarded the day before or the day after is not. An over-long framework has ONE defect -- its term -- and its call-offs are judged against the term it actually declares.

  • half_open_interval — not taken more conservative

    The window excludes the end date, so a call-off awarded on the framework's own last day is out of window. One day stricter, and it makes a zero-length framework incapable of any lawful call-off. No textual support for excluding the final day; it is the convention a reader who thinks in half-open ranges reaches for by habit.

  • window_capped_at_the_four_year_limit — not taken

    The window is the declared term intersected with the four-year limit, so every call-off made in the fifth year of an over-long framework carries an out-of-window defect ON TOP OF the framework's term defect. Attractive as a way to make the four-year rule bite on call-offs; double-counts one wrong as two, and makes the call-off determination depend on the term determination, which this domain otherwise keeps separate.

  • no_award_window_rule — not taken

    Art 33 imposes no award window and the limb does not exist. A call-off awarded years after the framework expired is judged only on the closed-parties and terms limbs. Textually arguable -- the window is read out of a DEFINITION -- and it leaves the four-year rule with no effect at all on call-offs, which cannot be right.

reopening_availability_under_a_complete_terms_framework EXPANSION

NOT YET REVIEWED — no qualified person has signed off this reading.

question
Art 33(4) says a several-operator framework "shall be performed in one of the following ways", and its point (c) authorises a reopening "where not all the terms ... are laid down in the framework agreement". May a contracting authority whose framework DOES set out all the terms, and which never stipulated the Art 33(4)(b) partly-with-partly-without possibility, nevertheless reopen competition among the parties?
chosen
closed_list_of_three_routes
rationale
Chosen as the conservative reading and on the chapeau's own words. Art 33(4) is drafted as an exhaustive trichotomy and point (b) exists precisely to give an authority the option the rival would grant for free -- if reopening were always available under a complete-terms framework, point (b) would have nothing to do, because its whole content is permission to mix the two routes plus the documentation duties that come with that permission. The rival's practical merit is real and is why this is registered rather than assumed: an authority that competes what it could have awarded directly harms nobody. A reviewer overturning this entry changes ONE branch of the route rule and one limb; the Art 33(5) limbs then apply to the newly lawful reopening automatically, because they are scoped on the ROUTE.
authority
none

Readings

  • closed_list_of_three_routes — TAKEN more conservative

    It may not. "One of the following ways" is exhaustive: (a) is a direct award, (b) needs the stipulation in the procurement documents, and (c) is conditioned on NOT all the terms being laid down. That combination therefore falls under none of them and the route is `no_lawful_route`, carrying the limb `reopening_not_available_where_framework_sets_out_all_terms`. Strict, and it condemns a practice that is arguably MORE competitive than the direct award the framework did authorise.

  • reopening_always_permitted — not taken

    It may. A reopening is more competitive than a direct award and cannot prejudice the operators, so nothing in Art 33 forbids an authority entitled to award directly from competing the contract instead; point (c) describes the case in which a reopening is COMPULSORY, not the only case in which it is permitted. The route is a lawful `multi_operator_reopened_competition` and the Art 33(5) procedural limbs apply to it. Comfortable in practice; it reads "shall be performed in one of the following ways" as if it said "may".

question
Art 33(2) third subparagraph forbids a call-off entailing "substantial modifications to the terms laid down in that framework agreement". Is substantiality here the same test as Art 72(4)'s -- the four in-any-event limbs about admitting other candidates, the economic balance, the extension of scope and the replacement of the contractor -- to be run inside this domain, or is it a judgement this domain takes as given?
chosen
atomic_input_here
rationale
Chosen as the conservative reading, and on the ground that the rival asserts an identity the text does not: a call-off is a contract MADE UNDER the framework, not a modification OF it, so Art 72(4)'s definition is at best an analogy. Taking the judgement as an atomic input is the same posture this domain takes to Art 33(1)'s "duly justified exceptional case" and Art 33(5)(b)'s "sufficiently long" -- it hands open texture to the user rather than inventing a crisp test. RESIDUAL HOLE, stated rather than hidden: this leaves the single most consequential input in the domain entirely unverified, and a caller with an interest in the answer supplies it. A reviewer who overturns this should expect the change to reach the profile contract, not just a clause.

Readings

  • atomic_input_here — TAKEN more conservative

    `call_off_entails_substantial_modification` is a single boolean the caller supplies, and this domain neither derives it nor second-guesses it. The Art 33(2) limb fires on it directly. Keeps the two articles' determinations separate and does not silently import a test the Directive states for a different question (modification of a contract DURING its term, not the making of a contract UNDER a framework). The cost is stated in the Art 72 excluded_set entry: a caller who answers it wrong gets a confident wrong verdict and nothing here can detect it.

  • delegate_to_art_72_substantiality_limbs — not taken

    The four Art 72(4) limbs are run against the call-off and the framework as the reference contract, and the Art 33(2) limb fires where any of them holds. Gives the caller a real test instead of a boolean, and gives the two articles one coherent notion of substantiality. But it imports four more atomic inputs (each of the Art 72(4) limbs is itself open-textured), it duplicates eu/procurement/contract_modifications, and it asserts an identity between two provisions that the Directive does not state -- Art 33(2) says "substantial modifications to the terms", Art 72(4) defines when "a modification of a contract" is substantial, and a call-off is not a modification of the framework at all.
